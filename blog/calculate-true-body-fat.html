22 How to Calculate Your True Body Fat Percentage From Multiple Methods

You have three numbers: BIA says 22%, skinfold says 19%, Navy says 24%. Which one is right? Probably none of them. But the average might be close. I’ve been in this situation hundreds of times. A client comes in with a folder full of printouts from their smart scale, their gym’s BIA machine, and an online calculator. Each number is different. They want to know which one is “true.” I tell them the truth: none of them is perfect, but together they tell a story. The Body Fat Percentage Estimator can combine multiple methods.

Why average methods Each method has error. The error can be random or systematic. BIA’s error is random with respect to hydration – sometimes high, sometimes low, depending on how much water you drank. Skinfold error is more systematic – if you have a consistent technique, the error direction is the same each time. Navy method error is also systematic – it tends to overestimate lean people and underestimate fat people. By averaging, you cancel out some of the random error. By weighting methods by accuracy, you reduce systematic error. Weighted averaging formula

Give more weight to more accurate methods. Based on the accuracy hierarchy: DEXA: weight 1.0

7-site skinfold (skilled technician): weight 0.8 Professional BIA (8-electrode): weight 0.6

3-site skinfold (skilled): weight 0.5 Consumer BIA scale: weight 0.3

Navy method: weight 0.3

If you have DEXA, you don’t need to average. That’s your number. If you don’t have DEXA, use the methods you have access to. A client named Mike had consumer BIA (22%), 3-site skinfold (19%), and Navy (24%). Weighted average = (0.3×22 + 0.5×19 + 0.3×24) / (0.3+0.5+0.3) = (6.6 + 9.5 + 7.2) / 1.1 = 23.3 / 1.1 = 21.2%. His true body fat was likely around 21-22%. The Skinfold Measurement Tracker can help you log your skinfold readings consistently.

When to trust one method over others If you have a DEXA, trust it. It’s the most accurate accessible method. If you don’t have DEXA but you have a skilled skinfold technician, trust that over BIA. Skinfolds give regional data and are less affected by hydration. If you only have a consumer BIA scale, trust it only for trends, not absolute numbers. A 5% swing day‑to‑day is normal. A 5% trend over three months is meaningful. If you only have the Navy method, it’s better than nothing, but consider investing in calipers or a DEXA. Pick two or three methods and use them consistently. Don’t compare BIA from one brand to skinfolds from a different technician. Use the same machine, the same protocol, the same time of day. A client named Sarah tracked her progress using consumer BIA and skinfolds.

Her BIA showed a 2% drop in body fat over six months. Her skinfolds showed a 3% drop. The absolute numbers were different, but the direction was the same. That’s what matters. The confidence interval approach

Instead of a single number, think of a range. A 20% body fat reading from a consumer BIA scale with 6% error means your true body fat is likely between 14% and 26%. That’s a huge range. Not very useful. A DEXA with 2% error gives a range of 18-22%. Much more precise. A weighted average of three methods with different errors gives a narrower range than any single method alone. What to do with the number Use it as a baseline. Track changes using the same method combination. Don’t chase absolute numbers. Chase trends. If your weighted average was 25% last year and 22% this year, you’re making progress. That’s true regardless of whether the absolute number is off by a point or two. A real example A client named Tom came to me with three methods: consumer BIA at 28%, Navy at 25%, and skinfolds at 24%. Weighted average: 26%. Six months later, after training and nutrition changes, his BIA was 24%, Navy 22%, skinfolds 20%. Weighted average: 22%. He lost 4% body fat. He didn’t care about the absolute numbers. He cared about the change. The bottom line

No single method is perfect. Each has blind spots. By combining methods, you get a clearer picture. The Body Fat Percentage Estimator does the math for you. Don’t obsess over the absolute number. Track the trend. Your body is changing. Make sure your measurment method can see it.
